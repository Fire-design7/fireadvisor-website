A fire alarm system is installed once but has to work every day for years. Detectors collect dust, the panel's batteries age, and refurbishments change the rooms. That's why the system is serviced regularly and every service visit is documented.

**In short**

- An installed system has to be kept in working order for as long as the facility operates.
- Servicing covers the control panel, detectors, manual call points, sounders and power supply.
- Every service visit is documented with a record kept together with the [fire safety file](/en/blog/pozharno-dosie).
- Maintenance is carried out by a company holding a permit from the National Fire Safety Directorate (GDPBZN).

## Why maintenance matters so much

A fire alarm doesn't put out a fire. It's there to warn people in time. If a detector is dusty, a battery is flat or a zone is disabled, the system may fail exactly when it's needed. The opposite problem is serious too: frequent false alarms teach people to ignore the sounder.

How the system works and what it consists of is explained in [How a fire alarm system works](/en/blog/pozharoizvestyavane).

## What servicing involves

The exact scope depends on the type of system (conventional or addressable) and the manufacturer's instructions. Usually the following are checked:

- **Control panel:** display, visual and audible signals, event log, recorded faults.
- **Power supply:** mains power and the standby batteries that keep the system running during a power cut.
- **Detectors:** activation with test equipment, contamination, damage, correct placement.
- **Manual call points:** accessibility and activation.
- **Sounders and visual alarms:** audible and visible in every zone.
- **Links to other systems,** if any: for example shutting down ventilation or releasing doors in case of fire.

At the end, a **service record** is made: what was checked, what faults were found and what was fixed.

## Why the records get checked

During an inspection, the authorities look not only at whether the system works right now, but also at whether there is proof that it's maintained. Service records are part of the facility's fire safety documentation. Without them, the system looks unmaintained on paper, even if it works.

So keep the records in one place, together with the fire safety file. If you're not sure what you have, take the [2-minute readiness test](/en/proverka-ot-pozharnata#test).

## The most common problems we see

- **A panel fault that has "just been there" for months.** The buzzer is silenced but the fault isn't fixed.
- **Flat standby batteries.** The system works while there's power, but stops during an outage.
- **Detectors covered during works.** Dust caps go on and nobody takes them off.
- **New walls and ceilings without detectors.** After a refurbishment, some rooms are left unprotected.
- **False alarms** from dusty detectors or the wrong detector type for the room (for example a smoke detector next to a kitchen).
- **Missing records.** The system was serviced, but there's no document to show it.

## What you can do yourself

Between service visits, the owner or the responsible person can keep an eye on a few simple things:

- Look at the control panel display. It should be in normal mode with no faults.
- Don't silence a fault signal without telling the maintenance company.
- Don't cover or paint over detectors.
- Don't put goods or furniture in front of manual call points.
- Let the maintenance company know before any works or changes to the rooms.

## How to choose a maintenance company

Ask yourself:

- Does the company hold a **GDPBZN permit** to maintain fire alarm systems?
- Does it know your system type and manufacturer?
- Do you get a **service record after every visit**?
- Will it tell you clearly what's wrong and what fixing it costs?
- Can it respond to a fault between scheduled visits?
